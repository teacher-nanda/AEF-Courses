📐 Grammar

Present perfect + for / since

Study how the present perfect is used with for and since.

They've known each other for ten years.

Julia's had that bag since she was in college.

A How long have you worked here? B Since 2010.

A How long has your brother had his motorcycle? B For about a year.

  • We use the present perfect + for or since to talk about something that started in the past and is still true now.
  • We use How long…? + present perfect to ask about an unfinished period of time (from the past until now).
  • We use for + a period of time (for two weeks), and since + a point in time (since 2016).
⚠️ Typical mistake — don't use the simple present with for / since.

They know each other for a long time. → They've known each other for a long time.

💭 Check your understanding

  • "They've known each other for ten years." Do they know each other now? (yes)
  • When did they meet? (ten years ago)
📐 Grammar

Present perfect continuous

Study how the present perfect continuous is formed and used.

have / has been + verb + -ing

How long have you been learning English?

Nick has been working here since April.

They've been going out together for about three years.

A Your eyes are red. Have you been crying? B No, I've been chopping onions.

  • We use the present perfect continuous with for and since with action verbs (learn, work, go) for actions that started in the past and are still true now.
  • With nonaction verbs (be, have, know) we use the present perfect simple, NOT the continuous.
  • We also use it for continuous or repeated actions that have been happening very recently — usually just finished, or with visibly present results.
⚠️ Typical mistakes

They've been knowing each other for ten years. → They've known each other for ten years.

I am working here for two years. → I've been working here for two years.

How long have you learning English? → How long have you been learning English?

live and work

live and work are often used in either the present perfect or present perfect continuous with the same meaning: I've lived here since 2010. = I've been living here since 2010.

Strong adjectives

With strong adjectives like exhausted, you can use absolutely, completely, really, or totally — but not very (NOT very exhausted). We often give strong adjectives extra stress.

💭 What do they mean? exhausted = very tired · freezing = very cold · filthy = very dirty

An informal email

Imagine you have friends in the US and you stayed with them for a week last month. Write an email to say thank you, using the plan below.

Your plan — one paragraph for each:

  1. Apologize for not writing before.
  2. Thank them for your stay.
  3. Talk about the nice things you did together.
  4. Talk about what you've been doing recently.
  5. Thank them again and invite them to stay.
  6. Send greetings to another member of the family.
