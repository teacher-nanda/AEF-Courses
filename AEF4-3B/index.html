📐 Grammar

What do adverbs tell us?

Adverbs and adverbial phrases can be one word (often) or several words (once a week). Different types tell us different things about a sentence.

🚶 Manner 🔁 Frequency 🕐 Time & Place 📊 Degree 💬 Comment

🚶 Manner

HOW something happens.

She walked quickly.

🔁 Frequency

HOW OFTEN something happens.

I rarely cook.

🕐 Time & Place

WHEN or WHERE something happens.

We arrived yesterday.

📊 Degree

HOW MUCH, or to what extent.

It's extremely cold.

💬 Comment

The speaker's OPINION about the whole sentence.

Luckily, nobody was hurt.

📐 Grammar

Where do they go?

Now look at where each type is normally positioned in a sentence.

🚶 Manner

Usually right after the verb / verb phrase.

He answered the question carefully.
She sings beautifully.

Passive verbs: mid-position — after the auxiliary: The cake was carefully decorated.

🔁 Frequency

Before the main verb, but after the verb to be.

I usually walk to work.
He's never late.

💡 sometimes/usually/normally can also start the sentence: Usually, I don't mind the rain.

🕐 Time & Place

Usually at the end of the sentence. If both appear, place comes before time.

They'll meet us at the station tomorrow.

💡 Time adverbs can also start the sentence: Tomorrow, we start our new jobs!

📊 Degree

Before the adjective or adverb it modifies.

I'm completely exhausted.
That's incredibly kind of you.

nearly / almost go before the verb: I nearly missed the bus.
a lot / much go after the verb: He travels a lot.
a little / a little bit can go with an adjective or a verb: We're a little tired.

💬 Comment

Usually at the beginning of the sentence or clause.

Fortunately, the flight wasn't delayed.
Apparently, the shop has closed down.

Other comment adverbs: luckily, basically, clearly, obviously, eventually…

📌 Other adverbs: Most other short adverbs (just, even, probably, actually…) go in mid-position — before the main verb. I just need five more minutes. She'll probably call later.
