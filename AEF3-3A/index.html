Myth or fact?

Read each statement about roads and driving and decide: is it a myth or a fact? Click your answer, then check — and see if any of them surprise you.

1. The world's longest traffic jam lasted more than ten days.
2. Traffic lights were invented after cars became popular.
3. Most of the world's population drives on the right.
4. The average car spends most of its life parked.
5. Red cars get more speeding tickets than cars of any other color.
  • 1. Fact. A jam on a highway near Beijing in August 2010 is reported to have lasted around twelve days, with vehicles moving only a few miles a day. Drivers ended up buying food from local sellers who walked between the cars.
  • 2. Myth. The first traffic signal was set up outside the Houses of Parliament in London in 1868 — it was gas-lit and was there to control horse-drawn traffic, decades before cars were common.
  • 3. Fact. Roughly two thirds of the world's population lives in countries that drive on the right, though around 75 countries — including the UK, India, Japan, and Australia — drive on the left.
  • 4. Fact. It's commonly estimated that a private car is parked about 95% of the time. Which raises a good question: do we really need to own one?
  • 5. Myth. This is a classic urban legend. There's no good evidence for it — and white, grey, and black are far more common car colors anyway.

What's the fastest way to get across Florida?

On Top Gear, a very popular TV series about cars and driving, they decided to organize a race across Florida to find the quickest way to cross a busy state.

The idea was to start from Miami, in the southwestern part of the tip of Florida, and to finish the race at the southern-most point of the US, Key West, a trip of 160 miles. Three possible forms of transportation were chosen: a motorboat, a car, and a combination of transportation. One of the show's hosts, Rutledge Wood, took the motorboat and his colleague Adam Ferrera took the car (a 2010 Lotus Evora). Tanner Foust took different kinds of transportation. His trip involved getting a taxi to the airport where he flew in a seaplane. Then he rented a scooter.

  • 1. Where do they have to go from? Where to?
  • 2. What are the three methods of transportation?
  • 3. Which one do you think will be the fastest? Why?
  • 4. In what order do you think the other two will arrive? Why?
  • 1. They go from Jones Boat Yard on the Miami River to the southern-most point in the US, Key West.
  • 2. A motorboat, a car, and a combination of transportation including a taxi, an airplane, and a scooter.
  • 3 & 4. Your own predictions — you'll find out later!

Glossary

1 mile the unit of distance used in the US and the UK (=1.6 kilometers); 160 miles = 257 kilometers

seaplane an airplane that can take off from the water or the land

no-wake zone an area of water where boats must travel slowly to avoid making waves

Rutledge in the motorboat

His trip began in the Miami River. For the first hour there was a speed limit of 7 miles an hour because of the enforced no-wake zone in the river. Once the boat passed through Biscayne Bay and went under the Rickenbacker Bridge, he entered open water. Rutledge increased the boat's speed to over 90 miles an hour and made up the time he lost on the Miami River. Approximately 60 miles from the finish line, Rutledge was ahead of Adam and Tanner, but he had to stop for gas if he wanted to reach the finish line in Key West. In the 15 minutes it took to refuel the boat, Adam passed Rutledge in the car and took the lead. Once Rutledge was back on the open water, there were a lot of big waves, but he was able to pass Adam near Seven Mile Bridge just outside of Key West. Unfortunately for both Rutledge and Adam, Tanner flew over both of them in the seaplane at about the same time. Now Tanner was in the best position to win. Each racer was only miles from the finish line in Key West. Who would win?

Adam in the car

Adam started off OK. He wasn't driving fast because he was going the speed limit. However, after an hour, Adam decided that he was going too slowly. He increased his speed to 75 miles an hour — 20 miles an hour over the speed limit. As Adam passed through a small town, he was stopped by the police! They were angry that Adam was speeding, and it meant that Adam lost many valuable minutes! Another problem Adam had was that his GPS was programmed to give directions in Spanish instead of English. This made finding his way to Key West difficult. As Adam finally approached Key West, the traffic was getting worse. He was worried about getting stuck so close to the finish line. Only three miles to go…

📐 Grammar

Comparatives

Study how we compare two people, things, or actions.

Comparing two people, places, things

My car is a little older than yours. · San Francisco is more expensive than Chicago.

This test is less difficult than the last one. · Olive oil is better for you than butter.

The new sofa isn't as comfortable as the old one. · I don't have as many books as I used to.

  • Comparative adjectives + than: old → older, big → bigger, easy → easier, modern → more modern, difficult → more difficult.
  • Irregular: good → better, bad → worse, far → farther / further.
  • One-syllable adjectives ending in -ed: bored → more bored, stressed → more stressed, tired → more tired.
  • We can also use (not) as + adjective + as to make comparisons.

Comparing two actions

My father drives faster than me. · He walks more slowly than I do. · Atlanta played worse today than last week.

Max doesn't speak English as well as his wife does. · I don't earn as much as my boss.

  • Comparative adverbs compare two actions: fast → faster, slowly → more slowly, carefully → more carefully.
  • Irregular: well → better, badly → worse.
💡 Object pronouns after than and as — She's taller than me. OR She's taller than I am. NOT She's taller than I. · They're not as busy as us. OR …as we are. NOT …as we.
the same as — Her dress is the same as mine.
📐 Grammar

Superlatives

Study how we compare something with all of its group.

Kevin is the tallest player on the team. · Singapore is the most expensive city in the world.

The small bag is the least expensive. · Lucy is the best student in the class.

Who dresses the most fashionably in your family? · That's the worst we've ever played.

  • We use superlative adjectives and adverbs to compare people, things, or actions with all of their group.
  • Form them like comparatives, but use -est instead of -er, and the most / least instead of more / less.
  • We usually use the before superlatives, but we can also use possessive adjectives, e.g., my best friend, their most famous song.
  • We often use a superlative with the present perfect + ever: It's the best book I've ever read.
⚠️ in after superlatives — use in (NOT of) before places after a superlative.

It's the longest bridge in the world. NOT of the world

It's the best beach in Florida. NOT of Florida

💭 Check your understanding

  • "Singapore is the most expensive city in the world." How many cities are we comparing it with? (all of them)
  • "My car is older than yours." How many cars are we comparing? (two)

What distracts drivers?

Read about the research, then number the eight activities from 1 (the most dangerous) to 8 (the least dangerous) — first your prediction, then check after listening.

Survive the Drive

Two scientists at Virginia Tech have published a book called Survive the Drive. In it, they describe their research on the most distracting things you can do when you are driving. They fitted video cameras and other devices to the cars of more than 3,500 drivers who were between the ages of 16 and 98. They then recorded their driving for three years and they got millions of hours of video. During this time, the drivers had more than 900 accidents, and the researchers used the video to analyze exactly why they happened.

Linking

Listen to the extracts from the interview and complete them with the linked words.

Linking

We often link words together in English, especially when we speak fast. We link words:

  • when a word ends in a consonant sound and the next begins with a vowel sound, e.g., the most‿interesting;
  • when a word ends in a consonant sound and the next begins with the same consonant sound, e.g., the worst‿time;
  • when a word ends in /t/ or /d/ and the next begins with /t/ or /d/, e.g., the biggest‿danger.

✍️ Writing

An article for a website

Write an article about transportation in your town or city, using a heading for each form of transportation.

Useful headings and questions:

  • Subway / Train — How much does it cost? Do you need cash or a card?
  • Buses — Where do you buy tickets? Are they a good way to see the city?
  • Taxis and ride sharing — Which is more expensive? Which is safer late at night?
  • Bikes and scooters — Is it the healthiest option? Are there bicycle lanes?
💡 Useful language — the cheapest way to get around is…, it's more expensive than…, the best option if you want to…, it takes about … to get from … to …

Getting around my city

The subway
The subway is the fastest way to travel across the city, and it's much cheaper than a taxi. You can buy a card at any station, and it takes about twenty minutes to get from my house to the center of town.

Buses
Buses are slower than the subway because of the traffic, but they're the best option if you want to see the city. Rush hour is the worst time to travel — everything is much more crowded.

Taxis and ride sharing
Taxis are the most expensive way to get around, but they're the safest late at night. Ride sharing is usually a little cheaper, though you need an app.

Bikes
Cycling is the healthiest option and there are more bicycle lanes than there were a few years ago — but it isn't as safe as it should be.
