Eat and drink — but at the right time!

In our super-busy 21st-century lives, most of us try to do as much as we can in 24 hours. However, we usually do things whenever it suits us, and science says this is disrupting our body's natural cycle, making us feel tired when we should feel awake, and vice versa. By doing things at the right time, we can be happier and healthier.

Have breakfast at 8:00 a.m. Researchers found that having breakfast at this time is good for our blood sugar levels. A research team from Sweden suggested that people who ate a regular breakfast as teenagers were 32% less likely to be at risk of heart disease as adults.

Don't drink coffee in the morning. Have one at 3:00 p.m. Early morning is the worst time to drink coffee because it stops the body from producing the stress hormone cortisol, which we need to help us wake up. The best time for caffeine is between 2:00 and 5:00 p.m., when it can make us more mentally alert.

Have lunch between 12:00 and 2:00. This is when our body digests food best, because the stomach produces the most acid. An experiment found that blood glucose levels after an evening meal were much higher than when people had the same meal earlier in the day — and high glucose can cause diabetes.

Have dinner at 7:00 p.m. Have your evening meal early. It should be delicious — our sense of smell and taste are at their best at this time — but light. Ideally, go to bed between 9:00 and 11:00 p.m., when the brain begins producing the sleep hormone melatonin.

  • 4. Why should an early evening meal be "delicious"?
  • 5. What's the best physical activity to do a) early in the morning, b) in the afternoon?
  • 6. Why does the body produce melatonin?

📐 Grammar

Simple present & present continuous

Study how the two present tenses are used.

Simple present — I live, he works

I live in New York. She works in a restaurant. We don't eat meat. Does the supermarket open on Sundays?

  • We use it for things that are always true or happen regularly.
  • Often with adverbs of frequency (usually, never) or expressions (every day, once a week).

Present continuous — be + verb + -ing

The phone's ringing. Can you answer it? House prices are going up very fast right now.

  • We use it (NOT the simple present) for actions in progress now or around now — usually temporary, not habitual.

Action and nonaction verbs

What are you cooking? It smells delicious. — I'm making pasta. — I love pasta.

  • Action verbs (cook, make) can be simple or continuous.
  • Nonaction verbs describe states or feelings and are not usually used in the continuous: agree, be, believe, belong, depend, forget, hate, hear, know, like, love, matter, mean, need, prefer, realize, remember, seem, suppose, want, and verbs of the senses (look, smell, taste, sound).
  • A few verbs can be both: I'm having lunch (action) vs. I have a cat (nonaction) · What are you thinking about? (action) vs. I think this music's great (opinion).
