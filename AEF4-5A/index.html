Survival: myth or fact?

Everyone thinks they know what to do in an emergency — but a lot of "survival advice" is completely wrong, and some of it is dangerous. Decide whether each one is a myth or a fact, then check.

1. If a snake bites you, you should suck out the venom.
2. If you're lost in the mountains, you should keep walking until you find help.
3. You can survive weeks without food, but only a few days without water.
4. You can always find north because moss grows on the north side of trees.
5. In cold water, the biggest danger in the first minute is not the cold — it's panic.
  • 1. Myth. Sucking a snake bite doesn't remove meaningful venom and wastes time. Keep the person calm and still, keep the bite below heart level if you can, and get medical help fast.
  • 2. Myth. The standard advice is the opposite: stay where you are. People who keep moving are much harder to find — and they usually end up more lost, more tired, and further from their last known position.
  • 3. Fact. The rough "rule of threes": you can last around three weeks without food, but only about three days without water. So finding water matters far more than finding something to eat.
  • 4. Myth. Moss grows wherever it's damp and shaded — that often happens to be the north side in the northern hemisphere, but it's unreliable enough that you shouldn't navigate by it.
  • 5. Fact. The first response to sudden cold water is an involuntary gasp and rapid breathing. If you panic and swim immediately you can breathe in water. The advice is to float, get your breathing under control, then act.
💡 Notice the language — If a snake bites you, you should… / If you panicked, you would… Hold on to that if — it's what this whole lesson is about.

"Survival can be summed up in three words — never give up. That's the heart of it, really. Just keep trying."

— Bear Grylls, adventurer, writer, and TV host

A 29-year-old doctor, was a participant in season five, in the "high earners" group.

Why did you decide to apply?
I'd always really enjoyed watching The Island with Bear Grylls. And I think it's really the ultimate , being stranded on a desert island, having to survive there with no help at all. What survival techniques did you learn?
So we learned a whole range of survival techniques. We learned how to make fire, which was actually quite complicated. You had to get the right wood from a particular type of tree on the beach, and then use pieces of that wood, and a shoelace to make fire. In our training, they made it look very — they had the fire lit within a few minutes. But in reality, it took us a couple of days before we made fire, but we did get it, which was amazing. Once we had fire, we could then boil water for drinking. The water we found was brown and green and had stuff floating in it, so we would filter it a pair of pants or a shirt to get rid of the big clumps of dirt, and then we would boil it to kill off any bacteria or parasites. Amazingly, nobody got from drinking the water during our whole five weeks on the island. They also taught us how to build shelters, how to navigate by the sun, and how to build up a map of the island as we explored it.

Before I went on the show, I said I'd miss my husband the most. But in fact, the thing I missed the most was most definitely food. It was all I could think about, and I couldn't sleep, I would go through a list of different pizza toppings in my head to try and get to sleep. I really missed having a good nights' sleep. we did build shelters off the ground to stop us from getting bitten by the insects, it was really uncomfortable. Having clean clothes, . Putting on dirty, wet socks every morning is one of the worst feelings. Of course, I missed my friends and family too, but actually what I realized was that I didn't miss many things. I didn't miss having a phone, or a computer, or the internet. Though as I said, I did miss clean, dry socks.

Lost in the Jungle

Four young men went into the Amazon jungle on the adventure of a lifetime. Only two of them would come out alive…

In 1981, three friends went backpacking in the Amazon rainforest in a remote area of Bolivia: Yossi Ghinsberg, 22, and his two friends Kevin Gale, 29, and Marcus Stamm, 29. They hired an experienced guide, an Austrian named Karl Ruprechter, who promised that he could take them deep into the rainforest to an undiscovered Indian village. Then they would raft nearly 125 miles back downriver. Karl said that the journey to the village would take them about seven days. Before they entered the jungle, the three friends made a promise that they would "go in together and come out together."

The four men set off from the town of Apolo and soon they had left civilization far behind. But after walking for more than a week, there was no sign of the village, and tensions began to appear in the group. The three friends started to suspect that Karl, the guide, didn't really know where the Indian village was. Yossi and Kevin began to get fed up with their friend Marcus because he was complaining about everything, especially his feet, which had become infected and were hurting.

Eventually, they decided to abandon the search for the village and just hike (instead of rafting) back to Apolo, the way they had come. But Kevin was furious because he thought that it was Marcus's fault that they had had to cut short their adventure. So, he decided that he would raft down the river, and he persuaded Yossi to join him, but he didn't want Marcus to come with them. Marcus and Karl decided to go back to Apolo on foot. The three friends agreed to meet in a hotel in the capital La Paz in a week.

Early the next morning, the two pairs of travelers said goodbye and set off on their different journeys…

📐 Grammar

Second conditional

Study how the second conditional is formed and used.

if + simple past, would / wouldn't + base form

If there was a fire in this hotel, it would be very difficult to escape.

I wouldn't have a car if I didn't live in the suburbs.

If it wasn't raining so hard, we could get to the top of the mountain.

If I were you, I'd make Jimmy wear a helmet when he's riding a bike.

  • We use second conditionals to talk about a hypothetical or imaginary situation in the present or future and its consequences.
  • In the if-clause you can also use the past continuous. In the other clause you can use could or might instead of would.
  • With the verb be you can use was or were for I, he, and she. But in conditionals beginning If I were you… to give advice, we always use were.

💭 Check your understanding

  • "If I won the lottery, I'd travel the world." Have I won the lottery? (no — it's imaginary)
  • Is it about now/the future, or the past? (now / the future)
📐 Grammar

Third conditional

Study how the third conditional is formed and used.

if + past perfect, would / wouldn't have + past participle

If they had found the river sooner, they would all have survived.

I wouldn't have gotten lost if I hadn't taken the wrong path.

He would have died if he hadn't been wearing a helmet.

If the weather had been better, I might have arrived earlier.

  • We use third conditionals to talk about a hypothetical past situation and its consequences — a situation that didn't actually happen.
  • You can also use the past perfect continuous in the if-clause. You can use could have or might have instead of would have in the other clause.
  • In the past perfect, had can be contracted to 'd, e.g., If they'd found the river sooner…

What really happened?

"If Kevin hadn't looked for Yossi, Yossi would have died." → In reality, Kevin did look for Yossi, so Yossi didn't die. The third conditional imagines a different past.

💭 Check your understanding

  • "If Kevin hadn't looked for Yossi, Yossi would have died." Did Kevin look for Yossi? (yes)
  • Did Yossi die? (no) — Is this about the past? (yes)
📐 Grammar

Second or third conditional?

Compare the two conditionals, then study the note.

1 If you came to class more often, you would probably pass the exam.
= You don't come to class enough. You need to come more often if you want to pass the exam. (present / future — second conditional)

2 If you had come to class more often, you would probably have passed the exam.
= You didn't come to class enough, so you failed. (past — third conditional)

🔀 Mixed conditionals — We sometimes mix second and third conditionals if a hypothetical situation in the past has a present / future consequence, e.g., You wouldn't be so tired if you had gone to bed earlier last night. / If he really loved you, he would have asked you to marry him.

A blog post

How to stay safe when you're hiking alone
Hiking alone can be wonderful, but you need to be prepared. First of all, always tell someone where you are going and when you expect to be back. Make sure you take plenty of water, a fully charged phone, and a map — don't rely only on your phone's signal. If you do get lost, the most important thing is to stop walking and wait to be rescued. Above all, don't panic: stay calm, keep warm, and trust that help will come.
